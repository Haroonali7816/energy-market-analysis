# Renewable Growth and Electricity Price Dynamics in Germany, 2015–2026

*An analysis of SMARD (Bundesnetzagentur) generation, load, and day-ahead price data*

## Overview

Germany's renewable electricity generation has expanded substantially over the past decade, driven primarily by growth in wind and solar capacity alongside the retirement of nuclear and, increasingly, coal generation. This analysis examines whether that expansion has measurably affected two aspects of the day-ahead electricity market: price volatility and the frequency of negative pricing events, in which wholesale prices fall below zero.

The data covers hourly generation (by source), load, and day-ahead price from December 2014 through August 2026, sourced from SMARD, the public market data platform operated by Germany's federal grid regulator.

## Key Findings

**Renewable share has grown from a minority to a majority of generation.** Averaged across the full study period, renewables accounted for 47% of German electricity generation. In the most recent twelve-month window (through August 2026), that figure reached 64%. Because 2026 data covers only through mid-August and solar output is seasonally concentrated in summer months, this figure is likely modestly inflated relative to a complete calendar year; a fairer comparison against the equivalent January–August period in 2025 is recommended before treating 64% as a stable baseline.

**Negative-price events have become substantially more common, broadly in line with renewable growth.**

| Year | Hours with negative day-ahead price |
|---|---|
| 2018 | 27 |
| 2019 | 211 |
| 2020 | 298 |
| 2021 | 139 |
| 2022 | 70 |
| 2023 | 300 |
| 2024 | 457 |
| 2025 | 573 |
| 2026 (through August) | 396 |

Negative-price hours roughly doubled between 2020 and 2025, consistent with the expected mechanism: as renewable capacity grows, periods of high wind or solar output combined with low demand more frequently exceed what the market can absorb at a positive price. The exception is 2021–2022, when negative-price hours fell even as renewable share continued to rise — addressed below.

**Price volatility rose sharply in 2021–2022, but the driver appears to be external, not renewable growth.** The standard deviation of hourly day-ahead prices trends upward across most of the study period in a pattern that loosely tracks renewable share. In 2021 and 2022, however, volatility spikes well beyond what the longer-run trend would predict. This period coincides with the European energy crisis triggered by the disruption of Russian gas supply following the invasion of Ukraine — a continent-wide market shock unrelated to any change in Germany's domestic generation mix. Attributing this spike to renewable growth would misread the data; a more defensible reading is that the gas crisis temporarily became the dominant driver of price behavior, independent of and larger in magnitude than the renewable-related trend.

**The two 2021–2022 anomalies are consistent with a single underlying explanation.** Elevated wholesale prices during the gas crisis raised the effective price floor across nearly all hours, making it less likely that any given hour — even one with high renewable output — would clear below zero. This is consistent with negative-price hours falling in 2021–2022 while volatility rose sharply: the same scarcity-driven price pressure that suppressed negative pricing also produced larger price swings. Negative-price frequency resumed its upward trajectory once the crisis eased, rising from 300 hours in 2023 to 573 in 2025.

**Negative pricing follows a seasonal pattern consistent with a supply-driven mechanism.** Negative-price hours are not evenly distributed across the calendar; they cluster in specific months associated with higher solar output and lower demand. This seasonal pattern is stable across the full study period, including through the 2021–2022 anomaly, supporting the interpretation that the underlying oversupply mechanism remained intact even in years when the annual count of negative-price hours temporarily declined.

**The generation mix shift underlies all of the above.** Wind and solar's combined share of generation grew steadily over the study period, while coal, lignite, and nuclear generation declined; Germany's last nuclear plants were retired in April 2023. This compositional shift is the structural change that produces the renewable-share trend reported throughout this analysis, rather than a separate or independent finding.

## Limitations and Data Quality Notes

**Nuclear generation records extend past Germany's confirmed shutdown date.** SMARD generation data for nuclear sources shows nonzero values as late as January 2024, roughly nine months after the April 2023 shutdown of Germany's final nuclear plants. This has not been fully resolved and may reflect residual decommissioning activity, a data reporting artifact, or a source-labeling issue. Findings specific to nuclear generation after April 2023 should be treated as provisional.

**Day-ahead price reflects a combined Germany-Luxembourg market, not Germany in isolation.** SMARD reports day-ahead price for the coupled "DE-LU" bidding zone rather than a Germany-only price. This analysis treats the DE-LU price as representative of the German market, a standard and reasonable simplification given the size of the coupled zone relative to Luxembourg's market, but it is not a literal Germany-only figure.

**A percentage-based measure of negative pricing (share of hours per year) shows a smoother upward trend than the raw hourly counts reported above, without the 2021–2022 decline.** This discrepancy has not been reconciled and may reflect differences in the total number of reported hours across years with varying data completeness, or an error in one of the two calculations. The raw counts, drawn directly from validated fact-table records, are treated as authoritative in this analysis; the percentage-based measure should be reconciled against them before being cited independently.

**2026 figures are based on a partial year of data** (through mid-August) and should not be directly compared to complete prior years without adjustment.

## Conclusion

The data support a genuine relationship between renewable generation growth and the increasing frequency of negative-price events, with a plausible and seasonally consistent mechanism behind it. The relationship between renewable growth and overall price volatility is weaker and less direct: volatility is shaped by multiple factors, and the 2021–2022 period demonstrates that an external market shock can temporarily dominate price behavior independent of the underlying renewable trend. A more complete model of price volatility would need to account for wholesale gas prices or another proxy for supply-side market stress, which falls outside the scope of the current dataset.
